# The Soldier Who Kept Fighting WWII Until 1974

Segmented narration. Each `---` line opens a segment: its ID, then the silence in
milliseconds to append after it. Synthesise each segment separately as plain text.
`pause_after` defaults to 400 where absent.

--- s01
On the ninth of March, 1974, a sixty-one-year-old bookseller flew from Japan to a small island in the Philippines, walked into the jungle, and read a short statement aloud to a man standing at attention in a patched uniform.

--- s02
The statement said that the Japanese Fourteenth Area Army had been dissolved. That all combat activity was to cease immediately. That the man was relieved of his duties.

--- s03 | pause_after: 900
The man listening was Second Lieutenant Hiroo Onoda. He was fifty-one years old. He had been fighting the Second World War, alone, for the last eighteen months, and with others for the twenty-seven years before that.

--- s04
He had heard, many times, that the war was over. He had read leaflets saying so. He had heard it from loudspeakers, from search parties, from his own family's voices played over the jungle on a recording. He had dismissed all of it.

--- s05 | pause_after: 900
What he had been waiting for was this. An order, from the officer who gave him the original one, delivered in person.

--- s06 | pause_after: 1200
That officer was a bookseller now. He had been found in a shop in Japan and asked to put the uniform back on.

--- s07 | pause_after: 2500
When he finished reading, Onoda took the bolt out of his rifle.

--- s08
Hiroo Onoda was born in March 1922, in a village in Wakayama Prefecture. Before the war he worked for a trading company in China, selling lacquerware. He was good at it. He had a gift for languages and an ordinary young man's life, and when he was drafted at twenty he was, by every measure, unremarkable.

--- s09 | pause_after: 900
What made him different was where he was sent to train.

--- s10
The Nakano School, in Tokyo, trained men for intelligence and unconventional warfare. Not infantry. Guerrilla operations, sabotage, intelligence gathering, and above all the doctrine of holding out behind enemy lines for as long as it took. The school taught its men that there were no circumstances in which they were to be captured, and no circumstances in which they were to kill themselves. Both were failures. Survival and continued resistance was the entire point.

--- s11 | pause_after: 1000
That training matters more than anything else in this story. Onoda was not a frightened conscript who got lost. He was a man specifically trained to do exactly what he then did, by an institution that considered doing it for decades a success rather than a tragedy.

--- s12
In December 1944 he was sent to Lubang Island, in the Philippines. The island is small, about thirty kilometres long. The Allied advance was already closing on the Philippines, and Onoda's orders were to destroy the island's airstrip and pier, to harass any landing, and to hold.

--- s13 | pause_after: 1000
His commanding officer was Major Yoshimi Taniguchi. The order Taniguchi gave him, before he left, was specific. Under no circumstances was he to surrender. Under no circumstances was he to take his own life. He was to hold out, however long it took, and Taniguchi would come back for him.

--- s14
Three months later the Americans landed on Lubang. The Japanese garrison was destroyed in days. Onoda took to the hills with three other men. Private First Class Yuichi Akatsu, Corporal Shoichi Shimada, and Private First Class Kinshichi Kozuka.

--- s15 | pause_after: 1600
Four men, in a jungle, on a small island, with an order to hold.

--- s16 | pause_after: 1200
The war ended five months later. Nobody on Lubang's hills believed it.

--- s17
Understanding why requires putting aside the obvious explanation. Onoda was not insane and he was not stupid. He was applying a piece of reasoning that was, given what he had been taught, almost airtight.

--- s18
He had been trained in deception operations. He knew, professionally, that an enemy will tell a holdout force that the war is over in order to flush it out. So every announcement that the war was over was, to him, evidence that someone wanted him out of the hills. The louder the announcement, the more effort behind it, the more it confirmed that he mattered enough to be worth deceiving.

--- s19
In late 1945, leaflets were dropped on the island carrying a surrender order from General Tomoyuki Yamashita. Onoda and the others examined them closely and found what they considered errors, phrasing a Japanese staff officer would not have used. They concluded the leaflets were forgeries.

--- s20 | pause_after: 1200
By their own logic, they were right to be suspicious. By any other measure, they had just talked themselves out of the end of the war.

--- s21
More came. Newspapers left in the jungle. Photographs. Letters from families. Loudspeakers. In one attempt, recordings of relatives' voices were played from aircraft. Onoda listened to his own brother's voice calling him and concluded the recording had been made under duress, or spliced, or imitated.

--- s22 | pause_after: 1600
There is no argument you can make to a man who has decided that every argument is part of the trap.

--- s23
So they stayed. They lived on bananas, coconuts, and rice they took from the island's farms. They killed cattle for meat. They built shelters in the rainy season and moved constantly in the dry. They maintained their rifles with palm oil and animal fat for twenty-nine years, and the rifles still worked at the end of it.

--- s24 | pause_after: 1400
And they fought. This is the part most retellings leave out, and it should not be left out.

--- s25
To Onoda, Lubang was an active theatre of war and its inhabitants were an enemy population. The farmers whose cattle he took were enemy. The police who came looking for him were enemy. Over the course of nearly three decades, his group is estimated to have killed around thirty Filipinos and wounded many more.

--- s26 | pause_after: 1200
They were not soldiers. They were islanders, farming an island they had always farmed, being shot at by men conducting a war that had ended before some of them were born.

--- s27 | pause_after: 2000
Any honest version of this story has to carry that. The loyalty people admire in Onoda and the deaths on Lubang are not two separate facts. They are the same fact.

--- s28
The group shrank. Akatsu, the youngest, walked away from the others in 1949 and gave himself up in 1950. The remaining three treated his defection as proof that he had been captured and turned.

--- s29 | pause_after: 1200
In May 1954, Shimada was shot dead in a clash with a Filipino search party on a beach.

--- s30 | pause_after: 1600
In October 1972, twenty-seven years after the war ended, Kinshichi Kozuka was killed in a firefight with Philippine police while the two men were burning a farmer's rice stores. Kozuka had been with Onoda since 1944.

--- s31 | pause_after: 2200
After that, Onoda was alone. He was fifty years old and he had been in the jungle for most of his adult life. He kept up the patrols. He kept the rifle clean. He kept, as far as anyone can tell, to the schedule of a soldier on active operations, with nobody left to see him do it.

--- s32
Kozuka's death put Onoda back in the Japanese newspapers. Search parties went out again and found nothing.

--- s33 | pause_after: 900
And then, in early 1974, a twenty-four-year-old university dropout named Norio Suzuki flew to the Philippines.

--- s34 | pause_after: 1200
Suzuki was not a soldier, a journalist, or a government official. He was a traveller, and he had set himself a list of three things to find. In his own description, he was looking for Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the Abominable Snowman. In that order.

--- s35
He went to Lubang alone, with camping gear. He did not bring a search party, a loudspeaker, or a weapon. He walked into the jungle and waited.

--- s36 | pause_after: 1600
On the twentieth of February 1974, after four days, Onoda found him.

--- s37 | pause_after: 1000
Consider that from Onoda's position. Twenty-nine years of search parties, armed patrols, aircraft, and broadcasts. And then one young man sitting alone in the jungle with no equipment and no backup, apparently wanting to talk.

--- s38
Onoda said later that Suzuki had come to the island to listen to the feelings of a Japanese soldier. Suzuki asked him why he would not come out.

--- s39
They spent days together. They became friends, which is a strange and very human detail. Onoda explained his position. He would not come out, because he had not been ordered out. He had received an order in 1944 from Major Taniguchi, and only Major Taniguchi could rescind it. Anything else was enemy deception.

--- s40 | pause_after: 900
Suzuki took photographs as proof and flew home.

--- s41 | pause_after: 1200
The Japanese government went looking for Taniguchi. They found him working as a bookseller.

--- s42 | pause_after: 1600
He was in his sixties. He had come home from the war, built an ordinary civilian life, and had almost certainly assumed that the men he left on Lubang were dead. He was now being asked to fly to the Philippines, put on his old uniform, and deliver an order thirty years late.

--- s43 | pause_after: 1400
He agreed.

--- s44 | pause_after: 1400
On the ninth of March 1974, in a clearing on Lubang, Major Taniguchi read the formal order rescinding the 1944 one. The Fourteenth Area Army was dissolved. All units were to cease operations. Onoda was relieved.

--- s45 | pause_after: 2000
Onoda described what happened in him in that moment as something coming apart. He had been carrying a mission for twenty-nine years and it was taken off him in under a minute.

--- s46
He handed over his rifle, his sword, around five hundred rounds of ammunition, and several hand grenades. The rifle was still in working order.

--- s47 | pause_after: 1400
Two days later, in Manila, he presented his sword to President Ferdinand Marcos, who pardoned him for the killings on Lubang.

--- s48
He flew into Japan on the twelfth of March 1974, and the country lost its mind.

--- s49
Crowds met him at the airport. He was on every front page. He was treated as a figure out of a vanished Japan. Proof, to some people, of a loyalty the post-war country was thought to have abandoned.

--- s50 | pause_after: 1200
He hated it almost immediately.

--- s51 | pause_after: 1000
The Japan he had been holding out for did not exist. He had left a militarised empire in 1944 and come back to a prosperous, democratic, American-allied consumer society in 1974. The country he had killed for had become something that had no particular use for what he had done. He was a celebrity, which is a different thing from being understood.

--- s52 | pause_after: 900
He wrote a memoir. And within a year and a half, he left.

--- s53
In 1975 he moved to Brazil, bought land, and became a cattle rancher. He married. He stayed for most of a decade, in a country with no opinion about the Imperial Japanese Army, doing physical work in a place where nobody recognised him.

--- s54 | pause_after: 1000
He came back to Japan in the 1980s after reading about a Japanese teenager who had murdered his parents, and he set up a nature school for children. An organisation that taught camping, survival, and self-reliance. He ran it for the rest of his life. Whatever he thought he had learned on Lubang, that was what he did with it.

--- s55 | pause_after: 1800
In 1996 he went back to the island. He met islanders whose families he had fought. His wife arranged a donation of ten thousand dollars to a local school. The visit was not universally welcomed, and it would be dishonest to present it as a reconciliation. For the families of around thirty people, no visit and no donation closes the account.

--- s56 | pause_after: 1600
Hiroo Onoda died in Tokyo in January 2014, of heart failure. He was ninety-one.

--- s57 | pause_after: 2400
Norio Suzuki, the young man who found him, who went looking for a lieutenant, a panda, and a yeti, found the panda. He died in 1986 in an avalanche in the Himalayas, still looking for the third.

--- s58 | pause_after: 1000
The thing that makes this story stick is not the length of time. It is that Onoda was, in his own terms, right.

--- s59 | pause_after: 1200
He was told to hold out until he was relieved. He held out until he was relieved. Every piece of evidence that the war was over came from a source his training told him to distrust, and the one source his training told him to trust was a man running a bookshop three thousand kilometres away who had no idea he was still alive.

--- s60 | pause_after: 1400
The system that put him there had a way in and no way out. It could issue him an order in 1944 and then simply stop existing, and nothing in it accounted for the man still standing at the far end of it.

--- s61 | pause_after: 1800
He was not mad. He was obedient, to an institution that had dissolved, in a war that had ended, on an island where people were still dying because of it.

--- s62 | pause_after: 3000
A bookseller had to get on a plane.
