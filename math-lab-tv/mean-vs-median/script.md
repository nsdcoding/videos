## Stress Test

- **s082** So which average is more trustworthy? Let's stress test them.
  - *Visual:* Two gauges labelled MEAN and MEDIAN, needles at 60 and 40.
- **s083** Suppose Pine Hill's richest family has an amazing year.
  - *Visual:* Mansion icon sparkles orange.
- **s084** Their income jumps from two hundred thirty thousand to two point three million. Ten times more.
  - *Visual:* Camera zooms out as the axis rescales to 0–2400 (ticks every 400); the orange dot slides to 2300.
- **s085** Nobody else's income changes at all.
  - *Visual:* Eight blue dots stay put, tagged 'unchanged'.
- **s086** The new total is two thousand six hundred ten. Divided by nine, the mean becomes two hundred ninety thousand dollars. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Fraction 2610 over 9 = 290; warn marker races right to 290.
- **s087** According to the mean, the typical family is now almost five times richer. Yet not one of the other eight got a raise. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* '≈ ×4.8' badge on the mean gauge; eight blue dots each show '+0'.
- **s088** And the median? Sort, cross off, and the middle is still forty. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* Violet marker stays at 40; median gauge needle doesn't move.
- **s089** The median doesn't care how far away an extreme value is, only which side of the middle it's on.
  - *Visual:* Left/right halves shaded; the 2300 dot just counts as '1 on the right'.
- **s090** Values that sit far away from the rest are called outliers.
  - *Visual:* Orange ring around the 2300 dot, label 'outlier'.
- **s091** The mean gets dragged toward outliers. The median hardly notices them. Statisticians call that being robust. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Tug-of-war: outlier rope drags the warn marker; violet marker anchored. Card 'robust = hardly moved by outliers'.
- **s092** But being robust has a cost. Let's go back to two hundred thirty thousand, and this time, the poorest family's income falls from thirty thousand to ten.
  - *Visual:* Axis zooms back to 0–240; orange dot returns to 230; the leftmost blue dot slides 30 → 10.
- **s093** Their life just got much harder. The mean dips, to about fifty-seven point eight.
  - *Visual:* Warn marker slides 60 → 57.8.
- **s094** The median stays at forty. It doesn't see the change at all. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* Violet marker frozen at 40; red label 'missed it'.
- **s095** The median ignores how far values are from the middle. The mean uses every bit of that. Each choice has a cost. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Two-column card: MEAN 'uses every value / dragged by outliers' — MEDIAN 'robust / ignores distance'.
- **s096** Now let's put everything back as it was, and step back to look at the shape of the data.
  - *Visual:* Poorest dot returns to 30; Maple and Pine Hill dot lines stack, top and bottom.
- **s097** Maple's incomes are spread evenly around the middle. The left side is a mirror image of the right.
  - *Visual:* Mirror line at 60 on Maple; left dots reflect onto right dots (−10↔+10, −8↔+8 …).
- **s098** We call that symmetric. And for symmetric data, the mean and the median land in the same spot. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Both markers overlap at 60; label 'symmetric: mean = median'.
- **s099** Pine Hill is lopsided: a crowd on the left, and a long, thin tail stretching off to the right.
  - *Visual:* Orange shaded arrow along Pine Hill from 48 to 230: 'long tail'.
- **s100** That's called right-skewed. And in right-skewed data, the mean usually gets pulled toward the tail, past the median. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Warn marker at 60 with an arrow toward the tail; violet marker at 40.
- **s101** Flip it, with the tail on the left, and the mean usually lands below the median.
  - *Visual:* Pine Hill's dot pattern mirrors horizontally on a schematic axis (labelled 'shape only'): tail now left, mean marker left of median.
- **s102** So here's a handy warning light: if the mean is much bigger than the median, a few large values are probably pulling it up. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Dashboard warning light: 'mean much bigger than median → look for big values'.
- **s103** One honest caveat: this is a rule of thumb, not a law. With oddly shaped data, it can fail. So always look at the actual values too. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* 'RULE OF THUMB' stamp lands on the warning light; small dot plot beside it.

## Which One to Trust

- **s104** So which average should you use? It depends on the question you're asking.
  - *Visual:* Road splits into two paths with signposts.
- **s105** If your question is about totals, use the mean.
  - *Visual:* Left signpost lights warn: 'TOTALS → mean'.
- **s106** Say Pine Hill's town council wants to plan a budget from a two percent local income tax.
  - *Visual:* Town hall icon; badge '2% tax'.
- **s107** Total income is the mean times the number of households. Sixty times nine is five hundred forty thousand.
  - *Visual:* 60 × 9 = 540 builds from nine blocks of 60.
- **s108** Two percent of that is ten thousand eight hundred dollars a year.
  - *Visual:* Coin stack counter → $10,800.
- **s109** And the richest family alone pays more than forty percent of it. The council needs that outlier counted. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Pie of the $10,800: orange slice '$4,600' (43%) vs blue rest.
- **s110** The median can't do this. Forty times nine is three hundred sixty, which misses a third of the real total. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Bar 540 (warn) next to bar 360 (violet); red gap '180 missing = 1/3'.
- **s111** But if your question is, what's life like for a typical family here, use the median.
  - *Visual:* Right signpost lights violet: 'TYPICAL → median'.
- **s112** If you move to Pine Hill, forty thousand is a far better guess for your neighbours' income than sixty. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* 'you' dot among the cluster; short gaps to neighbours at 40 vs long gaps at 60.
- **s113** And the mean and the median aren't the only options.
  - *Visual:* Shelf of three cards appears: mean, median, + 'others'.
- **s114** There's the mode, the value that appears most often. But no income in Pine Hill repeats, so here there isn't one.
  - *Visual:* Mode card; dots check for twins one by one: 'no repeats → no mode'.
- **s115** There's also the trimmed mean: drop the most extreme values at each end, then take the mean of what's left.
  - *Visual:* Scissors snip the 30 and 230 tiles off the row.
- **s116** Trim one from each end of Pine Hill, and the remaining seven add up to two hundred eighty. Divide by seven, and you get forty. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Fraction 280 over 7 = 40 (aqua).
- **s117** Here it happens to match the median exactly. In general, a trimmed mean sits somewhere between the two ideas: it uses most of the values, but one extreme one can't hijack it.
  - *Visual:* Slider from MEAN to MEDIAN with 'trimmed mean' in between.
- **s118** And when in doubt, report both the mean and the median. The gap between them tells you something too. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* 'mean 60 | median 40' side by side; aqua brace 'gap = 20 → skewed'.

  - *Visual:* Markers 'p50' (violet) and 'p99' (orange) on the axis; 99% of dots shaded left of p99.

  - *Visual:* Spreadsheet grid with Pine Hill's nine incomes.

- **s132** So data scientists compare the mean and median as a quick check for typos and outliers. And when filling in missing values in skewed data, they often use the median.

- **s133** Finally, machine learning. Here, the choice hides inside something called a loss function.
  - *Visual:* ML tile; card 'loss function = how we score mistakes'.
- **s134** Imagine a model that must make one single guess for every Pine Hill family's income. What's the best guess?
  - *Visual:* Guess slider (aqua line) on the Pine Hill number line.
- **s135** It depends on how you score the mistakes.
  - *Visual:* Two empty score panels appear.
- **s136** Score one: add up how far off the guess is for each family. That's called absolute error.
  - *Visual:* Plot: x 'guess (thousand $)' 0–120, y 'total distance' 0–800; distance lines from the guess to each dot.
- **s137** Slide the guess along, and the total distance is smallest at forty. The median. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Point traces the V-shaped curve; minimum at 40 (total 230) glows violet.
- **s138** Score two: square each distance before adding, so big misses count extra. That's called squared error.
  - *Visual:* Second plot: y 'total squared distance (thousands)' 0–70; squares drawn on the distances, equal x/y scale.
- **s139** Now the best guess is sixty. The mean. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Point traces the U-shaped curve; minimum at 60 (≈ 32.8 thousand) glows warn.
- **s140** This works for any data, not just Pine Hill. Minimising squared error lands on the mean. Minimising absolute error lands on the median, or with an even count, anywhere between the two middle values. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* Rule card: 'squared error → mean' / 'absolute error → median'.
- **s141** So when someone trains a model with mean squared error or mean absolute error, they're choosing whether it aims for a mean or a median.
  - *Visual:* 'MSE → aims for the mean', 'MAE → aims for the median'.
- **s142** Squared error chases outliers. Absolute error shrugs them off. Same trade-off, new setting. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Outlier dot yanks the MSE curve's minimum; MAE minimum stays put.

## Common Mistakes

- **s143** Before we wrap up, here are the three most common mistakes people make with averages.
  - *Visual:* Three red cards face down.
- **s144** Mistake one: taking the middle without sorting first.
  - *Visual:* Card 1 flips: 'Not sorting'.
- **s145** Here are Pine Hill's incomes in the order the houses sit on the street: forty-two, two hundred thirty, thirty-five, forty-eight, thirty, forty-five, thirty-eight, thirty-two, and forty.
  - *Visual:* Street of houses with tiles above, unsorted.
- **s146** The middle of that list is thirty. That's not the median. It's just whoever lives in the middle house. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Middle tile 30 circled red with a cross.
- **s147** Always sort first. Sorted, the middle is forty.
  - *Visual:* Tiles sort; 40 glows violet with a tick.
- **s148** Mistake two: assuming half the people are above the average.
  - *Visual:* Card 2 flips: 'Half are above average?'
- **s149** That's true for the median, by design. It's not true for the mean.
  - *Visual:* 'half above: median ✓, mean ✗'.
- **s150** In Pine Hill, only one family out of nine is above the mean. So if you're below average there, you're in very good company. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Pine Hill dots: 8 blue below the warn marker, 1 orange above.
- **s151** Mistake three: deciding the median is always better, and that outliers should just be deleted.
  - *Visual:* Card 3 flips: 'Delete the outlier?'
- **s152** That rich family is real. Their income is real money, and the town's tax budget depends on it.
  - *Visual:* Mansion and the $4,600 tax coin.
- **s153** Remove a value only when you have evidence it's an error, like that extra zero. Otherwise keep it, and choose the average that fits your question. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Checklist: 'evidence of error? → fix it. Otherwise → keep it'.
- **s154** And a bonus mistake: with an even count, picking one of the two middle numbers instead of the halfway point between them.
  - *Visual:* 40 and 42 tiles; red cross on '40' and '42', violet tick on '41'.

## Recipe

- **s155** Let's turn all of that into a recipe you can use on any set of numbers.
  - *Visual:* Recipe card frame unrolls.
- **s156** Step one: sort the values, from smallest to largest.
  - *Visual:* Item 1 writes on with a mini sorted row.
- **s157** Step two: find the mean. Add them all up, and divide by how many there are.
  - *Visual:* Item 2 with mini formula x̄ = Σx / n.
- **s158** Step three: find the median, the middle value. With an even count, take the halfway point of the middle two.
  - *Visual:* Item 3 with mini row and violet middle.
- **s159** Step four: compare them. If they're close, the data is probably fairly balanced. If the mean is much bigger, look for large values pulling it up.
  - *Visual:* Item 4 with tiny warning light.
- **s160** Step five: match the average to your question. Totals and fair shares: the mean. A typical person or thing: the median. Not sure? Report both. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* Item 5 with the two signposts.
- **s161** For Pine Hill, the mean is sixty, and the median is forty. Both are correct. They answer different questions. ⏸1.0s
  - *Visual:* Final card: 'Pine Hill — mean 60 (fair share) | median 40 (typical family)'.
- **s162** An average only lies when you ask it the wrong question. ⏸1.5s
  - *Visual:* Line types on in violet, centred.
