--- s01 | pause_after: 1400
Tsutomu Yamaguchi is the only person in history officially recognised as having survived both atomic bombs.

--- s02 | pause_after: 800
Not nearby. Not in the next town. Three kilometres from the centre of Hiroshima on the sixth of August, 1945, and three kilometres from the centre of Nagasaki three days later.

--- s03
He was not a soldier or a scientist. He was a twenty-nine-year-old engineer who designed oil tankers for a living. And the only reason he was alive to be in Nagasaki at all is that on the morning of the sixth, he turned back for a stamp.

--- s04
He had been in Hiroshima three months on a business trip for Mitsubishi. That morning was his last. He was walking to the tram with his bags when he realised he had left his personal seal at the office. The small carved stamp that, in Japan in 1945, served as a man's signature. Without it, no travel papers were valid. He could not get home.

--- s05 | pause_after: 900
So he turned around and walked back for it.

--- s06
That walk put him in a field on the edge of the city at fifteen minutes past eight in the morning, instead of on a tram heading into the centre.

--- s07 | pause_after: 1600
He noticed the aircraft first. A single bomber, very high up. Then something small falling beneath it.

--- s08 | pause_after: 2500
Then the sky went white.

--- s09 | pause_after: 2000
Three days later, four hundred kilometres away, in the middle of a sentence, it happened again.

--- s10
Yamaguchi was born in Nagasaki in March 1916, and by 1945 he had the kind of life that does not end up in history books. A draughtsman at Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. A wife, Hisako, and an infant son. A man who drew ships for a living and went home to his family.

--- s11
In the summer of 1945 Mitsubishi sent him to Hiroshima for three months. He travelled with two colleagues, Akira Iwanaga and Kuniyoshi Sato. They finished the work, and on the morning of August the sixth they were due to leave.

--- s12
The seal he went back for is worth understanding, because the whole story turns on it. A hanko is a small cylinder, usually wood or ivory, with a family name carved into one end. Pressed into red ink, it functions as a signature, and in 1945 it was not optional. Travel documents, company paperwork, bank forms. None of them were valid without it.

--- s13 | pause_after: 1200
So he walked back. That detour is the reason anyone knows his name.

--- s14
At fifteen minutes past eight, a B-29 called the Enola Gay released a uranium bomb over the centre of Hiroshima. It detonated roughly six hundred metres above the ground. The yield was about fifteen kilotons, the equivalent of fifteen thousand tons of high explosive. Around a hundred and forty thousand people would be dead by the end of the year.

--- s15 | pause_after: 1200
Yamaguchi was about three kilometres from the point directly beneath it.

--- s16
He described seeing the aircraft, and then two small objects drifting down. Then the light. He said it was like a magnesium flare going off in front of his face. He dropped to the ground. The blast wave lifted him, threw him, and he lost consciousness for a short time.

--- s17
When he came round, it was dark. Not night. The sky had filled with dust and smoke thick enough to blot out the morning. His left side was badly burned. Both eardrums had ruptured. He was temporarily blinded.

--- s18
He got up and walked toward what was left of the city, looking for his colleagues. What he walked through has been described many times by many survivors, and there is no version of it that is easy to hear. He found both men alive.

--- s19 | pause_after: 1200
And then he did the thing that makes this story what it is.

--- s20 | pause_after: 1800
He decided to go home.

--- s21
He spent that first night in an air-raid shelter with other survivors. Nobody in the shelter knew what had happened. There had been no siren worth the name, no formation of bombers, no sustained raid. One aircraft, one flash, and a city gone. People tried to explain it to each other with the only words they had, and none of the words fit.

--- s22 | pause_after: 900
Nobody in Hiroshima knew what an atomic bomb was. The term did not exist in their vocabulary that morning. Yamaguchi had been burned by a weapon that, as far as he understood, had no name.

--- s23
The next day he and his colleagues set out for the station.

--- s24
The trains were, improbably, still running. Hiroshima's rail links had been damaged but not destroyed, and in the days after the bombing people left the city in enormous numbers. For relatives, for hospitals, for anywhere else. To reach the station Yamaguchi had to cross the river. The bridges were down.

--- s25 | pause_after: 2200
He crossed on what was in the water.

--- s26
The journey to Nagasaki is a little over four hundred kilometres. Today it takes about two hours. In August 1945 it took the better part of a day, on crowded trains, through a country that was running out of everything.

--- s27
He made that journey with his left side burned raw, his arms wrapped in whatever cloth he had been given, deaf in both ears, travelling through a landscape that looked entirely normal. Fields. Stations. Other passengers. And he carried in his head something that nobody else on that train had any way of understanding yet.

--- s28
He reached Nagasaki late and went to a hospital, where a doctor he knew, a former classmate, treated his burns. By the accounts given later, his face and arms were so heavily bandaged that people who knew him did not recognise him. He went home. His wife, Hisako, thought at first that she was looking at a ghost.

--- s29 | pause_after: 900
He slept at home on the night of August the eighth.

--- s30 | pause_after: 1600
And on the morning of August the ninth, badly burned, deaf, bandaged from the shoulders up, Tsutomu Yamaguchi got dressed and went in to work.

--- s31
It is easy to make that sound like foolishness. It was not. In Japan in August 1945, you went to work. The country was in its fourth year of total war, food was rationed, cities were being firebombed nightly, and absence had to be explained. He was a Mitsubishi employee who had been away three months on company business, and he had to report what had happened to the Hiroshima office.

--- s32 | pause_after: 1200
So he walked into the Mitsubishi shipyard offices in Nagasaki and sat down in front of his supervisor.

--- s33
The supervisor's name was Sato. He listened to Yamaguchi describe what had happened in Hiroshima. One aircraft, one bomb, and a city of three hundred thousand people destroyed in a single moment.

--- s34 | pause_after: 1200
And he told Yamaguchi he was out of his mind.

--- s35
It is worth being fair to the man. What he was being told was, by every standard he had, impossible. He was looking at an employee who was burned, deaf, heavily bandaged and visibly in shock, describing something no bomb in the history of warfare had ever done. He asked Yamaguchi, reasonably, from where he sat, how a single bomb could destroy an entire city.

--- s36 | pause_after: 3000
Yamaguchi was still trying to explain when the room filled with white light.

--- s37
At two minutes past eleven on the morning of August the ninth, 1945, a B-29 called Bockscar dropped a plutonium bomb over the Urakami valley in Nagasaki. The yield was higher than Hiroshima's, around twenty-one kilotons. The hills around the valley contained the blast, which is the only reason the death toll was lower. Roughly seventy thousand people were killed.

--- s38 | pause_after: 1400
Yamaguchi was about three kilometres from the hypocentre. Again.

--- s39
The office windows blew in. He was thrown to the floor for the second time in three days, by the second atomic weapon ever used against human beings, while standing in a room trying to convince someone that the first one existed.

--- s40 | pause_after: 900
His bandages were torn away in the blast. He got up, and this time he knew exactly what had happened.

--- s41
Hisako and their infant son, Katsutoshi, had been at home when the bomb went off. Yamaguchi went to find them. He searched the ruins of the Urakami district street by street, not knowing whether they were alive.

--- s42
They were. Hisako had taken the baby out that morning to look for burn ointment, for her husband's injuries from Hiroshima. Being out of the house is what saved them. They were caught in black rain, but they were alive.

--- s43 | pause_after: 2200
His injuries from the first bomb are the reason his wife and son survived the second one.

--- s44 | pause_after: 1600
Six days later, the war ended.

--- s45
Most versions of this story end at Nagasaki, as though the remarkable part were the surviving. The remarkable part is the sixty-five years afterwards.

--- s46
He was ill for a long time. His hair fell out. His burns took years to heal properly. He was deaf in one ear for the rest of his life.

--- s47
And then he did something very ordinary. He worked as a translator for the American occupation forces in Nagasaki. Later he taught at a junior high school. In the early 1950s he and Hisako had two daughters. He returned to Mitsubishi. He drew ships again.

--- s48
In 1957 the Japanese government formally recognised the survivors of the bombings, the hibakusha, and issued them certificates. Yamaguchi's named Nagasaki. Only Nagasaki. Hiroshima was not on it.

--- s49 | pause_after: 1400
He did not correct it. Not for fifty years.

--- s50
Part of the reason was practical. Being certified for one bombing already entitled him to medical care and an allowance, and adding the second changed nothing on paper. But the larger reason, by his own account, was that he felt comparatively well. He had survived. He had raised children. There were people far worse off than he was, and he did not think his case needed attention.

--- s51
There is also something people outside Japan tend not to know. Being hibakusha carried a stigma. Survivors were treated as contaminated. Refused jobs, refused marriages, suspected of passing something on to their children. For decades, many said nothing at all. The silence was not modesty. It was self-protection.

--- s52 | pause_after: 900
So he stayed quiet about it until he was nearly ninety.

--- s53
What changed, by most accounts, was his son. Katsutoshi, the baby his wife had carried out of the house on the morning of the ninth, died of cancer in 2005, at fifty-nine. Hisako died in 2008, of liver and kidney cancer, at eighty-eight. Both had been exposed at Nagasaki.

--- s54 | pause_after: 1200
After that, Yamaguchi began to talk.

--- s55
In his eighties he wrote a book about what had happened to him. He took part in a documentary about niju hibakusha, double survivors, which was screened at the United Nations, and he travelled there to speak himself. He gave interviews. He wrote tanka, short Japanese poems, about the bombings.

--- s56
In March 2009, at ninety-three, Nagasaki city officials certified him for Hiroshima as well. He became the only person on record officially recognised as a survivor of both.

--- s57
He was asked why it mattered so late. He said that having it in the official record meant it could still tell younger people what the bombings had been, after he was gone.

--- s58 | pause_after: 1800
He said something else, too. What he hated about the atomic bomb, he said, was what it does to the dignity of human beings.

--- s59 | pause_after: 1200
An estimated 165 people experienced both bombings. He is the only one the record names.

--- s60 | pause_after: 2400
Tsutomu Yamaguchi died of stomach cancer in Nagasaki on the fourth of January, 2010. He was ninety-three years old.

--- s61
There is a version of this story that gets told as a piece of trivia. The unluckiest man in the world, or the luckiest, depending on who is telling it. It usually runs about ninety seconds and ends at Nagasaki.

--- s62 | pause_after: 900
That version is not worth much.

--- s63
What happened to Tsutomu Yamaguchi was not luck in either direction. He was in Hiroshima because a company sent him there. He was in a field instead of the city centre because he had forgotten a stamp. He was in Nagasaki three days later because it was his home and he went back to it, and because in August 1945 a man who had been away three months on business reported to his office.

--- s64 | pause_after: 1600
He spent the next sixty-five years not talking about any of it. He worked. He taught. He raised daughters. He buried a son. And at ninety-three, with almost no time left, he decided the record should say what had happened, so that it would still be there once he was not.

--- s65 | pause_after: 3000
He went back for his seal because without it, he could not go home.
